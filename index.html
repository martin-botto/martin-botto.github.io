The question

Project Harbor models a hypothetical Salesforce acquisition of HubSpot at $300 per share. The brief was to produce the kind of analysis a senior buy-side advisory team would prepare for a board: a clear recommendation supported by independent valuation methods, a synergy build, a financing structure, an EPS impact, and an honest risk register. The whole thing is a portfolio exercise, not a real transaction. Every number cited in the 20-page memo is reproducible in the accompanying Excel model.

Four methods, in plain language

Valuation triangulates four lenses. Public trading comparables benchmark HubSpot against twelve peer SaaS companies on revenue multiples adjusted for growth. Precedent transactions pull seven completed software acquisitions between 2019 and 2025 and run a regression of multiple against target growth rate. The discounted cash flow projects eight years of free cash flow at a 10 percent WACC and a 4 percent terminal growth rate, treating stock-based compensation as a real cash cost rather than an add-back. A premiums-paid analysis cross-checks the offer against historical premia in comparable deals. Each method has limitations, which is why all four matter.

The recommendation

The model lands at $300 per share, paid 70 percent cash and 30 percent stock, valuing HubSpot at $15.7 billion. The price sits below the DCF fair value of $322, below the comparable-company range of $320 to $462, and well below the $633 per share implied by the precedent-transaction regression at HubSpot's growth rate. Year-three synergies of $1.44 billion produce roughly $10 billion in net value creation after the premium paid, with one-time integration costs and a year of EPS dilution before accretion arrives in year two.

The question

Every subscription business loses customers, but spending money to keep them only pays off if you can answer three questions. How many are we losing? Which customers are about to leave, and why? And what is the return on stepping in? Project Anchor answers all three for a base of 7,043 customers. It finds where the losses concentrate, ranks which customers are most likely to leave, explains what is pushing them out, and puts a dollar figure on a focused retention program. Like the other studies here, it is a worked example built on a realistic, reproducible dataset modeled on a standard telecom customer file, so every number on this page is backed by the underlying analysis. The same approach runs without change on a company's real customer data.

Where the churn concentrates

Before any modeling, the customer segments already tell most of the story. Churn is overwhelmingly a new-customer, flexible-contract problem. A month-to-month customer leaves at 43.6 percent, while a two-year-contract customer leaves at just 3.0 percent. Anyone in their first year leaves at 51.5 percent, dropping to 5.5 percent after four years. Fiber-optic service and paying by electronic check both sit well above the 28.5 percent company-wide rate. In the chart, the dashed line is that company average, and the bars reaching past it are the pockets where retention money should go.

Predicting who will leave

The next step ranks every customer by how likely they are to leave. I compared two prediction models: a simple, explainable one and a more powerful pattern-finder. The number that matters here is not overall accuracy. A lazy model that simply assumes nobody leaves still looks 71 percent accurate, and is useless. What counts is how many of the customers who actually leave the model manages to flag, because a missed customer is lost revenue, while a false alarm costs only one retention offer. The explainable model flags 78 percent of the customers who go on to leave. The more powerful model is choosier but lets more than half of them slip through, so the explainable model wins for this job, and it has the bonus of showing its reasoning.

The grid below makes the trade-off concrete. Of the 502 customers who actually left, the chosen model correctly flagged 390 and missed 112. The 392 false alarms are the price of that reach, and at $120 an offer they cost far less than the revenue lost when a real leaver goes unnoticed.

Why they leave

The model also shows what pushes customers out the door, measured as how much each factor raises or lowers the odds of leaving. Fiber-optic service multiplies the odds of leaving by about 2.5 times, and paying by electronic check by about 1.9 times. Working the other way, a two-year contract cuts the odds by more than 90 percent, and adding tech support or online security cuts them by roughly 40 percent each. The point is practical, not academic: contracts, payment methods, and service bundles are all things a business can actually change.

The payoff: focused retention

A model is only worth something if it changes the budget. By ranking customers from most to least likely to leave and starting with the riskiest, the spend goes where the churn actually is. Contacting just the top 20 percent by risk reaches 46 percent of everyone who would have left, which is 2.3 times better than treating all customers the same. In the chart, the steeper the line climbs early on, the less money is wasted on customers who were never going to leave. The straight dashed line is what untargeted, random outreach would achieve.

Costed out across the whole base, the program clears a profit. Contact the 1,409 highest-risk customers with a $120 retention offer. On a conservative assumption that it saves 30 percent of them, that keeps about 278 customers worth roughly $734 a year each.

Recommendation

Retention is a targeting problem, not a blanket discount. The highest-risk customer is in their first year, on a month-to-month plan, with fiber service and electronic-check billing. The levers that work are contract incentives, moving customers off electronic check, and bundling in tech support and online security, each of which measurably lowers the odds of leaving. In practice: score the customer base regularly, send the riskiest group a costed retention offer, and require that every offer is justified by the revenue it protects. That turns churn from an unmanaged cost into a focused, profitable program, and the same approach runs unchanged on a company's real customer data.
